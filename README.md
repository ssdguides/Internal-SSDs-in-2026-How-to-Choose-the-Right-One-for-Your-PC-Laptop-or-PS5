Choosing an internal SSD isn’t about finding the drive with the biggest advertised speed. The right pick depends on what your computer supports, how much storage you need, and whether you’re reviving an older laptop, building a gaming PC, or moving large files.

Step 1: Understand four terms people mix up

Form factor: the physical shape (2.5-inch or M.2).
Interface: SATA or PCIe, which sets the bandwidth ceiling.
Protocol: AHCI for SATA, NVMe for PCIe.
PCIe generation: each step from Gen 3 to Gen 5 roughly doubles bandwidth, but your slot and CPU must support it.

M.2 is a form factor, not a speed. Some M.2 drives are SATA and some are NVMe, and a few slots accept only one type.

Step 3: Pick capacity honestly
500GB suits a light-use laptop. 1TB is the practical floor for most gamers and professionals. 2TB gives headroom for big libraries, and 4TB suits video or archives. Leave 10–20% free.

Step 4: Understand endurance (TBW)
TBW is the total data a manufacturer rates the drive to write under warranty. A 600 TBW drive works out to roughly 330GB written every day for five years, and typical home users rarely approach that.

Step 5: Plan cooling
Fast NVMe drives throttle when hot. Gen 5 drives usually need a heatsink, and thin laptops may leave no room for one, which favors cooler Gen 4 drives.

Step 6: Compare total value
Divide price by capacity for cost per terabyte, then weigh warranty, software and support, not just headline speed.

Is NVMe always faster in daily use?
On paper, yes. But booting Windows or opening a browser often feels similar on a good SATA drive. NVMe pulls ahead when you move large files, load big game assets, or edit high-resolution video.

Is Gen 5 worth it? For most people, not yet. It runs hotter and costs more, while Gen 4 already handles gaming and general use well.

Prices change often, so check the current listing before buying.
